# Interactions: what to always check, and how to grade

The goal is a short list the prescriber acts on. Detection is easy; triage is
the work.

## Grading defensibly

Databases disagree on severity, and their labels are not interchangeable. So
never write "moderate interaction" as though it were a property of the drug
pair — write which source graded it that way.

Then rank on three axes together, because severity alone produces the
forty-alert list nobody reads:

**Severity** — what happens if it is missed. Death or permanent harm at the top,
transient discomfort at the bottom.

**Likelihood** — does it actually occur at these doses, by these routes, in this
patient. Many documented interactions are dose-dependent, and many were
described with an interacting agent at a dose nobody uses.

**Actionability** — is there something the prescriber can do today. Separate a
drug, adjust a dose, monitor a level, or switch an agent.

A severe interaction with no available action ranks below a moderate one with a
clear fix. And a pair the patient has taken together uncomplicated for two years
is a different proposition from one being started today — **say which situation
you are looking at**, because it changes the recommendation from "stop" to
"continue and monitor".

State the action, not the alert. "Avoid combination" is less useful than
"separate doses by 4 hours" or "check potassium in 5–7 days".

## The always-check list

These deserve a specific look on every list, because the consequence is severe
and the combinations are common in ordinary prescribing.

**QT prolongation, stacked.** Individually modest QT-prolonging drugs are
additive, and the risk rises further with hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia,
bradycardia, female sex and structural heart disease. Common contributors span
macrolides and fluoroquinolones, azole antifungals, ondansetron, domperidone,
several antipsychotics and antidepressants, and some antiarrhythmics. Count how
many are on the list rather than assessing each alone, and check the potassium.

**Serotonin syndrome.** More serotonergic agents than people expect: SSRIs and
SNRIs, tramadol, linezolid, triptans, ondansetron, MAO inhibitors, and St John's
wort. Tramadol with an SSRI is the classic community-prescribed combination, and
tramadol also lowers the seizure threshold.

**Bleeding risk, additive.** An anticoagulant plus an antiplatelet plus an NSAID
is three mechanisms at once. Add an SSRI — which impairs platelet function — and
high-dose fish oil, and the total risk is far above any single component. Ask
about the supplements.

**The "triple whammy" and acute kidney injury.** An ACE inhibitor or ARB, plus a
diuretic, plus an NSAID. Each is reasonable alone; together they precipitate AKI,
particularly during any intercurrent illness with dehydration. Extremely common
and frequently missed because no single line looks wrong.

**Hyperkalaemia, additive.** ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics
including spironolactone, potassium supplements, trimethoprim, and heparin. Any
renal impairment amplifies all of it.

**Methotrexate.** Interacts dangerously with NSAIDs, trimethoprim and
co-trimoxazole, and proton pump inhibitors. Also — separately from interactions
— confirm it is prescribed **weekly**. Daily methotrexate dosing is a recurring
fatal error, and it is worth checking the frequency explicitly every time.

**Warfarin.** Interacts with a very large number of drugs, notably antibiotics,
azoles, amiodarone and NSAIDs. Any antibiotic course in a warfarinised patient
needs an INR plan, not just an interaction note.

**Statins with CYP3A4 inhibitors.** Clarithromycin, azole antifungals, and some
calcium channel blockers raise statin levels and rhabdomyolysis risk; the effect
differs sharply between statins, so name which statin.

**Drugs needing an acid environment, with acid suppression.** PPIs reduce
absorption of some antifungals, certain HIV drugs, and iron. A PPI on the list
is not benign background.

**Metformin and contrast, or acute illness.** Withhold around iodinated contrast
and during any illness causing dehydration, given the lactic acidosis risk in
renal impairment.

## Mechanisms worth recognising

Recognising the mechanism lets you predict interactions a database may miss —
particularly with newer agents and traditional preparations.

**CYP enzyme induction and inhibition.** Inducers — rifampicin, carbamazepine,
phenytoin, St John's wort — lower the levels of many drugs, and the classic
consequence is failure of hormonal contraception or of an anticoagulant.
Inhibitors — azoles, macrolides, some antivirals, grapefruit juice — raise them.
Onset differs: inhibition is fast, induction takes days to weeks and persists
after stopping.

**P-glycoprotein transport.** Affects digoxin and the direct oral
anticoagulants, and is a route by which amiodarone, verapamil and clarithromycin
raise levels.

**Absorption binding.** Calcium, iron, magnesium, zinc and antacids bind
tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones and thyroxine in the gut. The fix is usually
timing rather than avoidance, which makes it one of the most actionable findings
you can give — separate the doses.

**Additive pharmacodynamics.** No enzyme involved, just the same effect from
several drugs: sedation, anticholinergic burden, hypotension, QT, bleeding. These
are properties of the whole list, not of any pair, and they are what a
pair-by-pair checker structurally cannot see.

## Interactions that are usually noise

Reporting these unranked is how the real ones get ignored:

- Theoretical CYP interactions at doses where no clinical effect is documented
- Pairs the patient has taken together, stably, for years without incident
- Interactions requiring a route not being used — a systemic interaction flagged
  for a topical or ophthalmic preparation, though note that timolol drops are a
  genuine exception
- Duplicate alerts for the same underlying mechanism, listed once per pair
- Food interactions of no practical consequence at ordinary intake

These can go in a "reviewed, no action" section. That section has real value: it
shows the prescriber what was considered, which is what stops them re-checking
it themselves.

## What "no interaction found" actually means

It means either that the pair was checked and nothing was found, or that the
pair was never checked. Those are very different, and collapsing them is how a
review becomes falsely reassuring.

Say which. If a drug could not be resolved to a molecule, or a source could not
be retrieved for it, that drug is **unchecked** and belongs in the "not
verified" section by name — not silently in the safe pile.

Absence of a documented interaction is also weak evidence for newly marketed
drugs, where the interaction profile is still incomplete. Say so rather than
implying a clean result.
