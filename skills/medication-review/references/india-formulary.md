# Indian prescriptions: resolving what the patient is actually taking

The first step of any Indian medication review is working out the molecules,
and it is harder here than in most markets. This is where the review is won or
lost — every later check runs on the output of this one.

## Why brand-to-molecule resolution is the hard step

Indian prescribing is overwhelmingly by brand, and the brand landscape has three
properties that defeat recall:

**Brand names are reused across different molecules.** The same or a
near-identical brand name may be a different drug from a different manufacturer,
or a different molecule at a different strength within the same family. A brand
suffix — `-H`, `-D`, `-AM`, `-CT`, `-Plus`, `-Forte`, `-XR` — usually signals an
added component or a modified release, but the convention is not standardised
across companies.

**The strength is often the only thing distinguishing two products.** The same
brand at 5mg and 40mg can be entirely different formulations.

**Look-alike, sound-alike names are dense.** With tens of thousands of brands in
circulation, near-collisions are routine, and a misheard or mistyped brand
resolves cleanly to the wrong molecule.

The practical consequence: **do not resolve a brand from memory.** Look it up,
and if it cannot be resolved confidently, ask for the strip, the packet, or the
composition rather than guessing. A wrong molecule at step one makes every
subsequent finding wrong while looking entirely plausible.

## Fixed-dose combinations

India uses FDCs far more heavily than most markets. This is the single biggest
reason the molecule list is longer than the prescription.

**What it does to a review:**

- A four-line prescription can be nine or ten molecules.
- **Duplication becomes invisible at the brand level.** The commonest real
  finding in an Indian medication review is one molecule arriving twice through
  two different products, and no amount of brand-level checking will show it.
- Dose adjustment is constrained. If renal function requires reducing one
  component of an FDC, you cannot — the components move together. The fix is
  usually to split the FDC into separate agents, which is a prescribing change
  worth flagging explicitly.
- An FDC can hide a drug the patient should not be on at all — an NSAID inside a
  combination analgesic given to someone with chronic kidney disease.

**Common FDC families to expect:** antihypertensive combinations (an ARB or
ACE inhibitor with a thiazide, or with amlodipine), analgesic combinations
(an NSAID with paracetamol, sometimes with a muscle relaxant), antidiabetic
combinations (metformin with a sulfonylurea, a DPP-4 inhibitor, or an SGLT2
inhibitor), PPI-with-prokinetic capsules, and multi-ingredient cough
preparations.

**Banned FDCs.** The government has prohibited a large number of fixed-dose
combinations as irrational or unsafe, in several rounds, with some subsequently
contested in court. A prescription may still contain one — through old stock,
an old prescription being repeated, or a product whose ban is stayed. If an FDC
looks irrational, check its current regulatory status rather than assuming it is
legitimate because it was dispensed. Flag it to the prescriber.

## The paracetamol problem

Worth its own section because it is the most frequent avoidable overdose route
in an Indian medication list.

Paracetamol appears in combination analgesics, in cough and cold preparations,
and as a standalone PRN — and a patient can take all three without any single
line looking wrong. **Total the daily paracetamol across every product,
including PRN at maximum frequency**, and compare against the ceiling.

Two things raise the risk further: chronic alcohol use and low body weight both
lower the threshold for hepatotoxicity, and neither is usually on the
prescription.

The same additive logic applies to NSAIDs — an oral NSAID plus an NSAID inside a
combination plus a topical preparation is a real cumulative exposure, and it is
the renal and GI risk that accumulates.

## Drug schedules

Under the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules:

| Schedule | What it covers | Why it matters to a review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **H** | Prescription-only drugs | Should not be self-sourced, though often are |
| **H1** | Certain antibiotics, anti-TB drugs, some sedatives | Requires a separate register; signals a drug with resistance or dependence concerns |
| **X** | Narcotics and psychotropics | Stricter custody and records; dependence risk |
| **G** | Requires "to be taken under medical supervision" labelling | |

**Schedule H1 exists because of antimicrobial resistance and dependence.** An H1
antibiotic appearing without a clear indication, or an anti-TB drug in a list
with no TB diagnosis, is worth querying — partial or inappropriate anti-TB
therapy drives resistance and is a public-health issue, not only a patient one.

**Over-the-counter availability in practice differs from the schedule.** Many
prescription-only drugs, including antibiotics, are obtainable without one. So
"not prescribed" does not mean "not taken", and asking specifically is more
productive than assuming the list is complete.

## NLEM and price control

The **National List of Essential Medicines** feeds price control through the
NPPA's ceiling prices. Two things follow that occasionally matter clinically:

- A drug's price-controlled status can make a cheaper equivalent available,
  which is worth surfacing when cost is a barrier to adherence — and cost is a
  common, under-asked reason a patient has silently stopped a drug.
- Manufacturers sometimes move formulations out of controlled categories by
  altering the combination or strength, which is another route to a brand
  meaning something different than expected.

## What patients do not tell you they are taking

Routinely omitted when a patient lists their medicines, and routinely
interacting:

- **Over-the-counter analgesics** — the NSAID nobody counts
- **Ayurvedic, Siddha, Unani and homeopathic preparations.** Ask specifically.
  Some traditional preparations have documented heavy-metal content, and some
  interact directly. Patients frequently do not consider these "medicines" and
  will answer "no" to a general question about drugs.
- **Supplements** — high-dose fish oil adds bleeding risk, St John's wort is a
  potent enzyme inducer that lowers levels of many drugs, and calcium and iron
  bind several antibiotics and thyroxine at the point of absorption
- **Someone else's prescription**, shared within the family
- **Leftover antibiotics** from a previous course
- **Topicals and eye drops** — timolol drops are absorbed systemically and can
  cause bradycardia and bronchospasm

## Where to check

Prefer sources that are current and specific over recall:

- **The product's own package insert or the manufacturer's listing** — the
  authority for composition and strength
- **CDSCO** for approval status and banned FDC notifications
- **The National Formulary of India**, and NLEM for essential-medicine status
- **National programme guidelines** where relevant — the tuberculosis, HIV and
  malaria programmes set their own regimens, and a patient on programme therapy
  is on a protocol, not a free choice of drug
- **The hospital's own antimicrobial policy**, which overrides general advice on
  antibiotic choice because it reflects local resistance patterns

Whatever you use, name it in the output. An interaction severity or a renal
threshold means little without the source that graded it.
