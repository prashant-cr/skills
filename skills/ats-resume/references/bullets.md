# Writing bullets and summaries without inventing evidence

Read this before rewriting any resume content.

## The problem this file exists to prevent

Ask any language model to "make this resume stronger" and it will reliably turn

> Worked on the billing module

into

> Reduced billing errors by 35%, saving $200K annually

That output is better in every measurable way and it is a fabrication. Nobody checks it at the
resume stage, which is exactly why it is dangerous: it survives until an interviewer asks how the
35% was measured, and the candidate — who never claimed it — has to either bluff or admit the
resume overstates. Some of them will not find out until a reference check, after they have
resigned from something.

You are re-presenting someone's history. You do not have the facts to add to it, and the cost of
getting this wrong falls entirely on them.

## The shape of a bullet that works

**Action verb + what specifically + scope or outcome.**

Scope is the part people forget, and it is the escape hatch from fabrication. A bullet does not
need a percentage to be concrete — it needs to convey the size of the thing.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| Responsible for the billing module | Owned the billing module processing 40,000 invoices a month |
| Worked on the mobile app | Built the offline sync layer for an Android app with 200,000 installs |
| Helped with hiring | Interviewed 30+ backend candidates and wrote the screening rubric the team still uses |
| Involved in migration to AWS | Migrated 14 services from on-premise to AWS over two quarters |

Every right-hand example is verifiable by the candidate and invents nothing. The numbers are all
scope — counts of things that exist — rather than claimed improvements.

## Scope facts a candidate almost always knows

When a bullet has no number, one of these is usually available and needs no measurement
programme to answer:

- how many users, customers, invoices, tickets, requests, records
- how many people on the team, how many they led or mentored
- how many services, repositories, models, stores, markets, countries
- how long it took, or how often it runs
- the budget, the spend, the contract value
- what it was before: the runtime, the error rate, the manual hours

That last one is the most useful question in this whole file. "How long did it take before you
changed it?" turns a vague bullet into a real one, and the candidate always knows the answer.

## Asking for numbers so you actually get them

"Do you have any metrics for these?" gets "not really" from people with plenty, because it asks
them to do the analysis. Ask specifically, per bullet, and give them the unit:

> A few of your bullets would be much stronger with a number, and these are ones you probably
> know off the top of your head:
>
> 1. "Rebuilt the settlement pipeline" — roughly how long did the old one take to run, and the
>    new one?
> 2. "Managed the analytics team" — how many people, and over what period?
> 3. "Improved onboarding" — how many customers went through it a month?
>
> If you do not know one, say so and I will write it by scope instead.

That last sentence matters. It gives them permission not to guess, which is what stops a
well-meaning candidate from inventing a number themselves to be helpful.

## Verbs

Open with what the person did. The weak openers the scorer flags — `Responsible for`, `Worked
on`, `Helped with`, `Assisted with`, `Tasked with`, `Duties included`, `Involved in`, `Part of a
team that` — all describe an assignment rather than an action, and they read as a copied job
description.

Useful ranges, chosen by what actually happened rather than for variety:

- Built, Designed, Implemented, Shipped, Automated, Migrated, Rebuilt, Integrated
- Led, Mentored, Coordinated, Ran, Owned, Established
- Reduced, Cut, Increased, Accelerated, Consolidated, Recovered — **only with a real number**
- Analysed, Investigated, Diagnosed, Modelled, Forecast

Do not reach for a stronger verb than the truth supports. "Led" when they were one of five
contributors is the same category of error as a fabricated percentage, and it collapses under one
follow-up question in an interview.

## Length and count

Two lines maximum per bullet; one is often better. Three to five bullets for a recent, relevant
role, and one or two for jobs more than about eight years back — those exist to establish
continuity, not to be read.

If a role has eight bullets, the weakest four are hurting the strongest four. Cut rather than
compress.

## The summary

Three lines, no first person, no adjective the candidate cannot evidence. It answers: what kind
of professional, for how long, with what specialism, and what they are looking for now if that is
not obvious from the history.

> Data engineer with six years building batch and streaming pipelines on AWS. Owns the ingestion
> layer serving 40 analysts at a Series C fintech. Looking for a platform role with more
> ownership of reliability.

What to leave out, because it matches nothing a recruiter searches for and buys no credibility:
"results-driven", "passionate", "dynamic professional", "proven track record", "thinking outside
the box", "synergy". An objective statement about what the candidate wants from an employer is
also weak — the summary is the wrong place to negotiate.

For a career changer the summary is doing real work: it is the only place to explain the pivot
before the history confuses the reader. Say what they did, what they are moving to, and what
transfers.

## Keeping their voice

Match the register you were given. A resume that reads as fluent corporate English when the
candidate does not write that way sets up an interview mismatch, and the candidate has to live
inside the document you wrote.

Preserve their job titles exactly as their employer recorded them — titles get verified in
background checks, and "Senior Engineer" is not "Software Engineer III" even when it means the
same thing. If a title is genuinely misleading to an outside reader ("Ninja Growth Hacker"),
suggest a clarifying parenthetical rather than a replacement, and let them decide.
