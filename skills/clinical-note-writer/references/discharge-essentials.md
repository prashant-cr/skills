# The three sections that get omitted

Medication reconciliation, the follow-up plan, and safety netting. These are the
most-used parts of a discharge summary, the most common sources of preventable
post-discharge harm, and the three most likely to be reduced to a single line.

They are omitted for the same reason each time: the person writing the summary
knows all of it, so none of it feels like information.

## Medication reconciliation

A list of what the patient goes home on is only half of what the next
prescriber needs. **The other half is what changed and why**, because the GP is
about to reissue a repeat prescription from a list that predates the admission.

| Status | What it means | What it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Continued** | On it before, still on it | Nothing being dropped by accident |
| **New** | Started this admission | Ambiguity about who started it and why |
| **Changed** | Dose or frequency altered | Reverting to the old dose at the next repeat |
| **Stopped** | Discontinued, with the reason | The drug being helpfully restarted |
| **Time-limited** | Has a stop date | A 5-day course becoming a repeat |

**The "stopped, and why" row does the most work.** If the ramipril was stopped
because the creatinine rose, that reason has to travel with the list — otherwise
it reappears at the next repeat and the patient is readmitted. "Stopped" alone
is not enough; "stopped — AKI, recheck renal function before restarting" is.

**Time-limited courses need an explicit stop date, not a duration.** "Prednisolone
40mg, reducing by 5mg weekly, stop after 8 weeks" survives being read by someone
who does not know when it started. "Steroid taper" does not.

**Record what the patient was on before, if the source has it.** Reconciliation
means comparing two lists. Where the pre-admission list was never documented,
that is a gap worth naming — it is the commonest reason drugs silently disappear
across an admission.

Also worth capturing when present, and flagging when not:

- **Allergies and previous reactions**, with the reaction. "Penicillin allergy"
  meaning a childhood rash and meaning anaphylaxis are different facts.
- **Formulation specifics** where they matter — modified release versus
  immediate release is not interchangeable and the brand often encodes it.
- **Who supplies it** — whether the patient leaves with the drug in hand or a
  prescription to fill, which decides whether they get their first dose.

## The follow-up plan

"Review in OPD after 1 week" is the default, and it fails in four ways at once:
the patient does not know which department, the GP does not know what the review
is for, nobody knows who chases the pending biopsy, and if the appointment slips
there is no fallback.

A usable plan answers four things:

**Who** — the department, and a named clinician where continuity matters.

**When** — a date if one is booked, an interval if not, and **what to do if it
slips.** "If you have not been contacted within 2 weeks, call this number" is
the line that catches the patient the system loses.

**What for** — the question the review answers. "Review sputum culture and step
down antibiotics" tells everyone what has to be ready. It also lets a GP cover
it if the appointment is delayed.

**What must be checked, and when** — the monitoring that is easy to forget and
harmful to miss:

- Renal function and potassium after starting or increasing an ACE inhibitor,
  ARB, or spironolactone
- INR after any change near warfarin
- Full blood count on drugs that suppress marrow
- Levels for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs
- Blood glucose after a steroid course

**Pending results are their own line, with an owner.** A biopsy taken on day 3
and reported on day 10 belongs to somebody. Name who, or the result sits in a
system nobody is watching.

## Safety netting

The part most often absent entirely, and the one that decides whether a
deteriorating patient comes back in time.

Safety netting is telling the patient **what would mean this is going wrong, how
urgently to act, and exactly where to go**. It has to work at 2am, read by
someone frightened, possibly not the patient.

Three things make it usable:

**Specific to this condition.** "If you feel unwell, see a doctor" is not safety
netting. After a pneumonia discharge: breathing becoming harder at rest, fever
returning after settling, coughing blood, confusion, lips or fingers turning
blue. After abdominal surgery: pain suddenly worse rather than better, vomiting
and not passing wind, fever, the wound opening or discharging.

**Graded by urgency, with a destination for each.** People need to know the
difference between the three:

```
Come back immediately / call an ambulance     [the red flags]
Contact us within 24 hours                    [the amber ones]
Mention at the routine appointment            [the rest]
```

**Written as observable events, not clinical terms.** "Dyspnoea at rest" is not
usable at home. "Breathless when sitting still, or too breathless to finish a
sentence" is. The same applies in translation — a plain-language version in the
patient's own language is a rewrite at a lower reading level, not a word
substitution.

**Include where to go, by name.** "Go to the emergency department" is weaker
than naming the hospital and the department, especially where the patient was
admitted somewhere they do not live near.

Where the source notes do not contain any of this, do not compose it — the red
flags depend on the diagnosis and the clinician's judgement about this patient.
Put it in the gap report as something to add before signing, and say why it
matters.
