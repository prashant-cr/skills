## Ask about the use, not the specification

Someone asking which laptop to buy is asking because they do not know which specifications produce
the outcome they want. Asking them how much RAM they need hands the problem back.

Ask what runs on it, where, for how long, and what annoys them about the current one. Then you
choose the specifications, and you can explain the choice in terms they can check.

This is also the difference between a recommendation and a specification table. "Best value" has
no meaning until the use is pinned, because the cheapest adequate product is a different product
for every use.

## The core six

Establish these for any category:

1. **Market.** Country, and city if service coverage might matter. It changes price, availability,
   warranty and which sellers are safe. Default to India when nothing suggests otherwise, and say
   you are assuming it.
2. **What it is for**, concretely. Push past the category noun to the actual task.
3. **Budget**, and whether it is firm. Ask what they would pay if something clearly better sat
   just above — most people have a comfortable number and a real ceiling, and the gap is where the
   good answers live.
4. **What they own now, and why they are replacing it.** The reason is usually the real
   requirement. "It is slow" and "the battery is dead" lead to completely different
   recommendations, and neither is "I need a new laptop".
5. **How long they expect to keep it.** Decides whether ownership cost outweighs purchase price,
   and whether resale matters.
6. **Non-negotiables.** A port, a size, a weight, an ecosystem, a brand they will not buy, a
   colour someone else has opinions about.

## Asking well

**One block, not a sequence.** Six questions in one message get answered. Six messages get
abandoned.

**Say why, briefly.** "So I can rule out the ones that will not fit" earns better answers than
bare questions.

**Offer defaults where you can.** "I will assume India and a three-year keep unless you say
otherwise" lets them correct rather than compose.

**Do not re-ask what they told you.** If the request already contains the budget and the use,
confirm and ask only for the gaps.

**Stop at the blocking question.** If only the market is missing, ask for the market. Do not
deliver a form because a form is the pattern.

## Category-specific questions

The ones that actually change the answer:

**Laptops and phones** — what software, how many hours unplugged, do they carry it daily, screen
size preference, any ecosystem they are locked into, is repairability or resale a factor.

**Headphones and audio** — commute or desk or gym, noise cancelling needed or nice, wired
acceptable, glasses (changes clamp comfort), how long worn at a stretch, source device.

**Cameras** — what subject, video or stills, existing lenses, how much weight will actually be
carried, and whether a phone already covers most of it. This last one saves people a lot of money.

**Kitchen appliances** — household size, counter and cupboard space, how often it will genuinely
be used, and whether it will be washed by hand.

**Printers** — pages a month, colour needed, photos or documents, and whether third-party
cartridges matter to them. Volume decides ink versus laser and nothing else does.

**TVs** — viewing distance and room brightness, what they watch, whether it is for gaming, and
what audio they will use. Room brightness matters more than most specifications.

**Washing machines and large appliances** — load size, space and door-swing, water pressure and
inlet, floor type, service coverage in their city.

**Tools** — occasional or regular, existing battery platform, and how bad it is if it fails
mid-job. Occasional use is where cheap is genuinely correct.

**Vehicles** — distance a year, terrain, who else drives it, parking, fuel or charging access, how
long they keep vehicles.

**Anything wearable or fit-dependent** — return policy becomes a primary criterion, not a footnote.

## Questions that look useful and are not

- **"What brands do you like?"** Anchors them to what they already know and narrows the search
  before it starts. Ask about brands they will *not* buy instead — that is a real constraint.
- **Detailed specification preferences** from someone who is asking what to buy. If they knew,
  they would not be asking.
- **"What is your budget?" alone**, without asking whether it is firm. The single number is often
  a guess.
- **Long feature checklists.** People say yes to features that cost money and get used twice. Ask
  what they do, not which features sound good.
- **"How important is quality to you?"** Nobody says "not very".

## Reading what they did not say

- **The stated reason for replacing is often not the real one.** Someone replacing a laptop
  because it is "slow" may have a full disk or a dead battery. Worth one question — the cheapest
  recommendation available is sometimes a repair.
- **A very low budget for a demanding use** means something has to give. Say which, early, rather
  than delivering a recommendation that cannot do the job.
- **A very high budget for a simple use** deserves a note that they can spend less. People
  appreciate this more than the industry assumes.
- **Urgency changes the answer.** Someone who needs it tomorrow cannot wait for a sale, and
  telling them to is not advice.
- **Buying for someone else** changes everything about returns, fit and taste. Ask.

## When to stop asking

When you can name the constraint that decides the choice. That is usually after one round.

If most of it is there, **build the shortlist and state the assumptions**. A recommendation with
two clearly-labelled assumptions is more useful than a second round of questions, and the user can
correct an assumption in five words.

The exception is when a missing fact would make the entire answer wrong rather than approximate —
the market, usually, or a hard compatibility constraint. Ask for that one thing and nothing else.
