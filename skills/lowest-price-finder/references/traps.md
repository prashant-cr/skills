## The governing idea

**The more a price beats the market, the more evidence it needs.** This inverts
the instinct that sorting by price is doing the buyer a favour. Retail margins
on most goods are thin, so a seller 40% below everyone else is not simply more
efficient — something structural is different, and the buyer is entitled to
know what.

Sometimes the difference is benign: clearing old stock, a genuine sale, a lower
cost base. Often it is not. The job is to name which.

## Grey imports

Genuine product, imported outside the manufacturer's official channel. Common
in electronics, cameras, watches and cosmetics.

**Signals:** "imported", "international variant", "no local warranty", "seller
warranty", price well under every authorised seller, no GST invoice offered
(India), packaging described as "may vary", manuals in another language.

**What the buyer gives up:** manufacturer service, which matters most exactly
when the product fails. Also possible band/voltage/plug incompatibility,
software region locks, and no recourse in a recall.

**Verdict:** legitimate to buy knowingly. The saving is real and so is the
risk. Never let one win a comparison against warrantied stock without labelling
what it is — the buyer is comparing two different products.

## Counterfeits

Concentrated in cosmetics, perfume, batteries, memory cards, cables, audio,
supplements and branded clothing — light, high-margin, hard to tell apart.

**Signals:** a price too far below the authorised market; a seller name
unrelated to the brand with no history; stock photos only; broken-English
descriptions; the brand's own store not carrying that price; reviews mentioning
"not genuine", "different packaging", "stopped working"; memory or batteries
that "test" below rated capacity.

**Highest-risk purchase pattern:** a marketplace listing, sold by a third-party
seller, fulfilled by the seller rather than the platform, at a large discount.

**Verdict:** exclude. There is no saving in a fake, and for batteries, chargers
and supplements there is a safety argument as well.

## Seller vetting

On a marketplace, **the platform is not the seller**. The seller determines
authenticity, warranty handling and whether the parcel arrives.

Check:
- **Rating and count together.** 4.8 from 30 ratings is weaker evidence than
  4.3 from 40,000. Few ratings is as informative as poor ones — a new seller is
  unknown, not good.
- **Age.** A seller account created recently, listing a high-demand product
  below market, is the classic pattern.
- **Who fulfils it.** Platform-fulfilled (Amazon FBA, Flipkart Assured) means
  the platform holds the stock and returns are easy. Seller-fulfilled means
  neither.
- **Whether the brand recognises them.** Many brands publish authorised seller
  or dealer lists. One check settles it.
- **Return window.** No returns on an expensive item is a decision the seller
  made about their own product.

## Fake discounts

The "was" price is frequently fictitious. A ₹4,999 MRP that has never been
charged makes a ₹1,499 price look like 70% off when it is simply the price.

**How to tell:** check the product's own price history rather than its claimed
discount. Where a price-history tool exists for that market, use it; where it
does not, compare the "discounted" price against the spread of current sellers.
A real discount sits below the market, not merely below a number the seller
chose.

Related patterns: a discount that appears every week and is therefore the
normal price; countdown timers that reset; "only 2 left" that is always 2.

## Listing tricks

Things that change the real price without changing the displayed one:

- **"From ₹X"** — the cheapest variant, not the one the buyer wants
- **Shipping revealed at checkout**, sometimes after the address
- **Bundle mismatch** — no charger, no cable, no case, where a rival includes them
- **Quantity confusion** — a pack of one priced as if a pack of three
- **Renewed or open-box** shown in the same list as new, distinguished only by
  a small label
- **Cash-on-delivery surcharge**, or a fee for the payment method the buyer uses
- **Coupon required** — a price achievable only after clipping something on the page
- **Marketplace vs brand store** carrying different warranty on the same item

The fix for all of them is the same: read the actual product page for the
actual variant, and record shipping and fees explicitly rather than assuming
zero.

## Outright scams

**Red flags that should end the evaluation:**
- Payment demanded outside the platform, or by bank transfer, UPI to an
  individual, gift cards or crypto
- A store whose domain was registered weeks ago, with no returns address, no
  company details and no working contact
- Prices far below every legitimate seller across a whole catalogue, not one item
- Pressure mechanics and a checkout that collects unusual detail
- Copied product photography and text lifted from a known retailer

Never advise a buyer to pay outside a platform's protected flow to get a lower
price. The discount is the bait for exactly that instruction.

## Legitimate reasons a price is low

Not everything cheap is suspect. All of these are real and worth surfacing as
genuine savings:

- **A genuine sale** — festive, seasonal, clearance
- **Old stock** — previous generation, discontinued colour, last season's model
- **Open-box or customer return**, usually with full warranty
- **Manufacturer-refurbished**, often with warranty and at a substantial saving
- **A card or bank promotion** the buyer can actually use
- **Lower cost base** — a specialist retailer in its own category, or a direct
  brand store cutting the retail margin
- **Loss-leading** — a platform buying market share, common at launch and
  during sale events
- **Bulk or pack pricing** where the unit price genuinely falls
- **Short-dated stock** on consumables — fine if it will be used in time

The distinction that matters: legitimate low prices have an **explanation the
seller is happy to state**. Grey imports and counterfeits rely on the buyer not
asking.
