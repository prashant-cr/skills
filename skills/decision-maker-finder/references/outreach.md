# The first touch

Research is wasted if the opening reads like the nine other emails that person
deleted this morning. This is what to do with the map once you have it.

## Approach the champion, not the buyer

The instinct is to write to whoever can sign. It is usually wrong, for a
practical reason: **the economic buyer has no problem.** The person with the
problem is the one whose week is worse because of it, and they are typically
one or two levels below.

Three things follow.

- **The champion will tell you who the buyer is**, accurately and for free.
  That beats any external research, and it is the fastest way to correct a
  wrong committee map.
- **A referral downward carries weight; a referral upward carries very little.**
  If you must start senior, ask to be pointed to the right person rather than
  pitching — a CXO forwarding you internally is a strong opening, and the two
  sentences it takes are easy for them to say yes to.
- **Going straight to the CXO on a small deal signals you do not understand the
  company.** Deal size sets the altitude of the first email as much as it sets
  the committee.

The exception is the founder-run company under roughly 200 people, where the
founder genuinely is the buyer, the champion and the user. Write to them.

## What makes a first email land

The whole job is proving in two lines that this was written for one person.

- **A specific, verifiable observation about them or their company.** A job
  posting, a talk they gave, a launch, a migration, a regulatory deadline.
  Something you could only know by looking.
- **A relevance claim tied to that observation**, not to your product's feature
  list. "You are hiring three integration engineers" earns the next sentence.
- **One idea.** An email arguing three things is answered by none of them.
- **A small, concrete ask.** A specific question, or a named time. "Worth a
  chat?" transfers the work of deciding to the person with least incentive.
- **Short.** Under 120 words. If it needs scrolling on a phone it is competing
  with things they actually have to read.
- **A plain signature with a real identity and an easy way to be told no.**

**Say where you found them if it is not obvious.** It costs one clause, it is
required in several jurisdictions, and it reads as confident rather than
evasive.

## What marks an email as bulk

Recipients classify in about two seconds. These are the tells:

- **"I hope this email finds you well."** No further reading required.
- **Merge-field praise** — "impressive work at COMPANY" — that would be true of
  any company in the list.
- **A paragraph about your company before anything about theirs.**
- **Feature lists, and claims with no number or a fabricated one.**
- **Fake familiarity** — a "following up on our conversation" when there was no
  conversation. This one converts a non-response into active distrust, and it
  is the single most damaging pattern here.
- **Manufactured urgency** — a deadline the recipient can tell is invented.
- **Tracking pixels and link wrappers** on a first cold email, which trip
  filters and read as bulk infrastructure.
- **Sending to a role alias and a named person on the same thread.**

The compounding cost is deliverability. Bulk-classified mail trains the
recipient's filter against your domain, and that damage outlives the campaign
and affects the companies you researched properly.

## Timing and triggers

Timing beats persuasion. A well-researched email at the wrong moment loses to a
mediocre one that arrives during an active problem.

Strong triggers, roughly in order:

- **A new person in the seat.** The best trigger available. New arrivals
  reassess vendors in their first ninety days and are actively looking for
  ways to make an early mark. Watch leadership pages and announcements.
- **Funding raised.** Budget appears and gets allocated fast.
- **Hiring into the relevant function.** A job posting is a public statement
  that the company is investing in that area right now.
- **A compliance or regulatory deadline** in their industry.
- **A public failure** — an outage, a breach, a bad quarter attributed to
  something you address.
- **A competitor's win or loss** in their market.
- **A system migration or a renewal window** on the incumbent tool.

Weak triggers that people over-rate: a company anniversary, an award, a
generic "saw your post" on a post with four thousand likes.

## Multi-threading without being a nuisance

Deals with one contact die when that contact goes quiet. Deals with four
contacts and no coordination annoy everyone. The line between them:

- **Approach different people about different things.** The champion hears
  about the problem; the security reviewer hears about architecture and
  certifications; procurement hears about commercial terms. Sending the same
  email to three people in one department is the mistake.
- **Never make it a race.** Two people discovering they were pitched
  separately, with different framing, ends the deal and the relationship.
- **Reference the thread you already have.** "I have been speaking with Priya
  about X, and wanted your view on the security side" is legitimate and welcome.
  Concealing it is not.
- **Do not go over someone's head to pressure them.** Escalating past a
  champion who has gone quiet converts a slow deal into a lost one and costs
  you the internal advocate.
- **Cap the total.** Three or four people at a mid-size company is thorough.
  Ten is a campaign, and it will be discussed internally as one.

## The referral ask

The highest-yield message in cold outreach is not a pitch. It is a short,
easily-answered request for a pointer:

> I am trying to reach whoever owns [specific function] at [company] — is that
> you, or could you point me the right way? Happy to explain in two lines
> either way.

It works because it is genuinely easy to answer, it does not require the
recipient to evaluate anything, and a wrong-person reply is still a win. It
also fails safely: if they own it, you have confirmed the committee map from
the inside, which is the thing external research is least reliable at.

Use it whenever confidence in the map is *Inferred* rather than *Confirmed* —
it converts a research gap into a conversation instead of a wrong assumption.
