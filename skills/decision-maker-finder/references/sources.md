# Where to look, in order of what you can trust

The difference between a usable decision-maker map and a plausible-looking one
is almost entirely source discipline. This is the hierarchy, what each source
is genuinely good at, and where each one lies to you.

## The hierarchy

| Tier | Source | Trust | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Regulatory filings — listed companies, **and any company that has filed to list** | Highest | Senior management only |
| 1 | The person stating their own current role | Highest | Undated, and self-reported titles inflate |
| 2 | Company leadership / team page | High | Updated late; often only founders |
| 2 | Company job postings | High | Names a seat and a reporting line, rarely a person |
| 2 | Company press releases and newsroom | High | Skews to announcements, goes stale in place |
| 3 | Conference speaker lists, webinar panels, podcasts | Good | Coverage is patchy by industry |
| 3 | Trade press and industry publications | Good | Repeats the press release, including its errors |
| 4 | Company registries (MCA, Companies House, state filings) | Structural only | Statutory roles, not operating authority |
| 5 | Contact databases and aggregators | Low | Frequently stale, provenance often unclear |

**The rule that matters:** a claim from tier 4 or 5 alone is *Inferred*. Two
independent tier-2 or tier-3 sources that agree make it *Probable*. A tier-1
source dated within six months makes it *Confirmed*.

## The filing check, first

Before working down the hierarchy, spend two minutes establishing whether a
**prospectus, DRHP or annual report exists**. This is the highest-leverage
search in the whole process and it is routinely skipped for private companies,
on the assumption that tier 1 means "listed".

It does not. A company that has *filed to list* has published, under
prospectus liability, a document that names senior management with exact
titles and joining dates — plus the group structure, segment leadership,
headcount by function, customer concentration, and frequently a product
inventory that tells you whether they have already built what you are selling.

Search for it explicitly: `"<company>" DRHP`, `"<company>" prospectus`,
`"<company>" annual report`, and the exchange's own filing archive. Download it
and extract locally rather than relying on press coverage of it — the coverage
reports the headline numbers and omits the management annexures, which are the
part you need.

Two things this catches that nothing else does:

- **Whether the problem you are selling into already has an internal owner or
  an internal product.** Discovering their platform already ships the module
  you are pitching, *before* the first email, changes the entire approach.
- **Confirmed absences.** If no CISO or CIO appears anywhere in the Board, KMP
  or Senior Management list, that is a sourced finding rather than a gap in
  your research — and it tells you where security review actually sits.

## Company-controlled sources

The company's own site is the best starting point and the most commonly
skipped past.

- **Leadership / About / Team pages.** Authoritative when present, but most
  companies list only founders and CXOs, and pages are updated when someone
  joins far more reliably than when someone leaves.
- **The newsroom and press releases.** New senior hires are announced with an
  exact title and a date. This is the single cleanest way to date a role.
- **Job postings.** Chronically underused. A posting tells you a seat exists,
  what it owns, who it reports to, and that the company is spending in that
  area right now. When you cannot name a person, a posting still lets you name
  the seat and its reporting line — which is a legitimate deliverable.
- **Investor relations, annual report, and any regulatory filing.** For listed
  companies this is tier 1 and current.
- **The support, security or trust pages.** Often name the DPO, the security
  contact or the compliance owner — exactly the gatekeeper roles that kill
  deals and that nobody researches until it is too late.
- **Vendor / supplier onboarding pages.** If the user is selling, this is
  sometimes the company's own preferred front door, complete with a named
  procurement contact.

## The person's own statements

A professional profile the person maintains is strong evidence, with two
caveats worth stating in the report.

- **Undated by default.** "Current" on a profile means the last time they
  updated it. Cross-check against a company source.
- **Titles inflate.** Self-reported seniority runs ahead of internal grade,
  particularly in sales and consulting. Confirm scope — team size, budget,
  reporting line — rather than accepting the title string.

Read profiles normally, through the site or via Sales Navigator if the user
has a licence. Do not build a scraper: it violates the terms, it is enforced,
and it is not necessary for a single-company lookup.

Conference talks, podcast appearances and published articles are especially
good, because the person described their own remit in their own words, usually
with a date attached — and that description is exactly what you need to know
whether they hold budget.

## Third-party and press

Trade press is useful for two things specifically: **naming the seat holder at
a moment in time**, and **surfacing buying triggers** — a funding round, a new
plant, a compliance deadline, a system migration, a competitor's outage.

Its weakness is that it propagates the press release verbatim, so a wrong title
in the original appears in eight outlets and looks like corroboration. Two
outlets carrying the same wire copy are **one source**, not two. Check whether
they are genuinely independent before upgrading a grade.

## Contact databases

Apollo, ZoomInfo, Lusha, Clearbit and the rest are legitimate tools and worth
using — as a **starting hypothesis, never as the answer**.

- Coverage of India, and of mid-market companies anywhere, is materially worse
  than their coverage of US enterprise.
- Records persist long after roles end. A database entry with no "last
  verified" date is not evidence of anything.
- Direct-dial mobile numbers of unclear provenance are the part to be most
  careful with. If the user cannot say where a number came from, it does not
  go in the report.

**Always corroborate a database hit against a tier 1–3 source before grading
it above Inferred.** The database tells you where to look; it does not tell you
what is true.

## Registries outside India

The same structural-not-operational caveat applies everywhere: registries tell
you who is legally responsible, not who buys.

| Market | Registry | Gives you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **UK** | Companies House | Directors, PSCs, filings, accounts — free and current |
| **US** | State Secretary of State; SEC EDGAR for filers | Registered agents and officers; for SEC filers, real executives and compensation |
| **EU** | National business registers; EU Business Register portal | Directors and filings, varying depth by member state |
| **Singapore** | ACRA BizFile | Directors, shareholders, structure |
| **UAE** | Free-zone and mainland registries | Limited public access; often needs the free zone directly |
| **Australia** | ASIC | Directors and company extracts |

For US private companies, SEC EDGAR is useless but **state filings, trademark
records and court dockets** frequently name officers. For any market, the
**patent and trademark register** names inventors and counsel — a route to the
technical decision maker that almost nobody checks.

## Dating every claim

Attach a date to every fact, and prefer the date the source *reflects* over the
date you retrieved it.

- A leadership page has no date; infer one from the newest person listed or
  from the site's own change history.
- A press release has a clean date and is the best anchor available.
- A profile shows a start month; the absence of an end date is not proof of
  presence.

Report it as: **name, title, source, date the source reflects.** A user who can
see that the evidence is fourteen months old knows to make one confirming call
before investing in the account. A user handed an undated name does not.

## Departure signals

Before committing to a name, spend a minute looking for evidence the person
has gone. Cheap to check, expensive to miss.

- The leadership page no longer lists them, but an older cached version does
- Their profile shows a new employer, or the role now reads as ended
- A press release announces a successor, or an "interim" holder of the seat
- Their company email bounces on verification while colleagues' resolve
- Recent company communications are signed by someone else in that function
- A job posting exists for what looks like their role — the strongest signal
  of all, and the one that also tells you the seat is currently empty

An empty seat is genuinely useful intelligence, not a failure: it means the
budget likely still exists, the incumbent's decisions are up for revisiting,
and the person who gets hired into it will be looking for solutions in their
first ninety days. Report it as a finding with the timing implication attached.
