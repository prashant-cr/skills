# Finding the decision maker in an Indian company

India is the market where the naive method fails hardest. The public record is
unusually rich — every registered company files directors, financials and
charges with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs — and unusually misleading,
because the people in that record are frequently not the people who decide.

## The MCA director trap

Every private limited company in India must have at least two directors, and
every director appears in the MCA register with a DIN, a name and a date of
appointment. This is the most accessible "who runs it" data in the country and
the most consistently misread.

**A statutory director is a legal role, not an operating one.** In Indian
private companies the board very often includes:

- **Family members** appointed to satisfy the two-director minimum, sometimes
  with no involvement in the business at all
- **Nominee directors** placed by an investor to hold a board seat
- **Founders who have since left** operations but never filed a resignation
- **Professional directors** who sit on many boards and manage none of them

Meanwhile the person who actually approves your purchase — the VP of
Engineering, the Head of Marketing, the plant manager — will usually hold **no
directorship whatsoever** and will not appear in the MCA record at all.

**Use MCA to establish structure, not authority.** It answers: does this
company exist, how old is it, who owns it, what is the paid-up capital, is
there a parent, is it in default on filings, does it have charges registered
against its assets. Those are genuinely useful and hard to get elsewhere.

It does not answer who signs off on a ₹6 lakh software purchase. Only the
operational sources do.

**One important exception:** in owner-operated businesses — most Indian SMEs,
family manufacturing, distribution and trading firms — the managing director on
the MCA filing genuinely is the decision maker for almost everything. The
smaller and more traditional the company, the more the statutory record and the
real authority converge. Read the trap as: verify which kind of company this
is before trusting the register.

## What the public record actually gives you

| Source | What it is good for | What it will not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MCA21 / MCA master data** | Existence, CIN, incorporation date, registered office, directors with DIN, paid-up capital, filing status | Operating roles, anyone below the board |
| **Tofler, Zauba Corp, IndiaFilings** | The same MCA data made readable, plus financial summaries and charge history without a filing fee | Same limits — it is MCA underneath |
| **Annual return (MGT-7) and financials (AOC-4)** | Shareholding, related parties, auditor, revenue and profit for private companies | Anything current — filings run 6 to 18 months behind |
| **GST portal search** | Whether a GSTIN is active, legal name, registration state, taxpayer type | People |
| **IEC / DGFT** | Import-export activity and codes | People |
| **EPFO establishment data** | A rough headcount signal via employee contributions, useful when headcount is not disclosed | Names or roles |
| **SEBI filings and stock exchange disclosures** | For listed companies: genuinely current KMP, board committees, senior management with real titles | Nothing below senior management |
| **Startup funding coverage and investor portfolio pages** | Founders, senior hires, stage, recent raise — usually current | Middle management |

For a **listed** Indian company, the exchange filings are the best source in
any market — the KMP list, the investor presentation and the annual report name
real operators with real titles, and they are current. Start there and you may
not need anything else.

For an **unlisted** company, the filings establish the shell and everything
about the humans has to come from operational sources: the company's own site,
professional profiles, press, conference speaker lists, job postings.

**Job postings are underrated here.** An Indian company hiring a "Manager -
Marketing Automation, reporting to the AVP Digital" has just told you the
reporting line, the seniority ladder and the existence of a seat, for free and
with a current date. When the leadership page is empty, the careers page often
is not.

## Title inflation and what titles mean here

Indian corporate titles run senior relative to Western equivalents, and the
gradient is steep in services companies and banks.

- **"Director"** in an Indian IT services or consulting firm is commonly a
  senior individual contributor or a mid-level manager — not a board director
  and not a functional head. Check whether the person appears in the MCA
  director list to tell a statutory director from a job-title director.
- **AVP, VP, SVP** in Indian banking and financial services are often three to
  four levels below where the same title sits in a US company. A "VP" may
  manage a team of four and hold no budget.
- **"Head of"** is usually more meaningful than "VP" — it implies functional
  ownership rather than a grade.
- **"Business Head" or "Vertical Head"** in a large Indian company generally
  does carry P&L authority, and is frequently the real economic buyer for
  anything sold into that vertical.
- **CXO titles at companies under 50 people** are frequently co-founder titles.
  Treat them as founders, and expect founder behaviour: fast decisions, no
  process, and everything routed through one person.

The practical rule: **do not map an Indian title onto a Western org chart.**
Establish seniority from headcount managed, budget mentioned, reporting line in
job postings, and how the company itself describes the role — not from the
title string.

## When the website only says info@

Most Indian SME websites publish one generic address and a landline. This is
not an obstacle so much as a signal about how the company operates.

What works, roughly in order:

1. **The careers page and job postings.** Reporting lines, team structure, and
   often a named hiring manager.
2. **Professional networks, searched by company and function** rather than by
   the title you expect. Search the function, then read the titles you find.
3. **Industry association and trade body member directories.** Indian
   industries are densely organised — CII, FICCI, NASSCOM, and dozens of
   sector bodies publish member representatives by name.
4. **Trade press, event speaker lists and webinar panels.** Indian B2B
   conferences publish speaker names with exact titles and current dates. This
   is one of the highest-yield and most underused sources in the market.
5. **The switchboard.** Call, ask by function and not by name: "who looks after
   IT purchasing?" A receptionist who has not been given a name to protect will
   usually just tell you. This is legitimate — you are identifying yourself and
   asking a straightforward question. Inventing a pretext to get past them is
   not, and it is the line in the body of the skill.
6. **GST invoice contacts and vendor registration portals** where the company
   publishes a procurement contact for suppliers. If the user is selling, the
   vendor-onboarding page is sometimes the intended front door.

## Group and promoter structures

Indian business groups concentrate decisions in ways the org chart hides.

- **Shared services at the group level.** IT, HR, insurance and facilities for
  a dozen operating companies are frequently bought once, centrally. The
  subsidiary you are targeting may have no authority to buy anything.
- **The promoter family.** In promoter-led groups, purchases far below the
  threshold you would expect can require family sign-off, particularly anything
  touching finance, compliance or the group's external image.
- **The family office or holding company** often houses the real decision layer
  for strategic spend, and it is a separate legal entity you have to find
  separately.
- **Professional CEO with a promoter chairman** is the structure to read most
  carefully. The CEO holds the title; the chairman may hold the decision. The
  reliable tell is where the long-tenured finance and legal people report.

Trace ownership up through the MCA holding-company field before finalising who
the economic buyer is. It is ten minutes and it is the difference between
pitching the budget holder and pitching a cost centre.

## Names, and why they break email patterns

Indian naming conventions break first-dot-last logic more often than any other
major market, and `scripts/email_pattern.py` handles these cases explicitly.

- **Mononyms.** Many South Indian names have no surname in the Western sense.
  A person may be recorded as a single name across all systems.
- **Initial-prefixed names.** "S. Venkatesh" or "K. R. Ramesh" — the initials
  expand a father's name, a village or a house name, and the mail system may
  use the initial, drop it, or expand it. All three occur.
- **Very long surnames.** Some systems truncate. "venkataraman" becomes "venkat"
  or "venkatara" depending on the field length someone chose in 2011.
- **Order reversal.** Some organisations record surname first, and the email
  pattern follows the record rather than the spoken order.
- **Spelling variants.** Krishnan/Krishnann, Reddy/Reddi, Shah/Sah,
  Chatterjee/Chattopadhyay. Verify rather than assume, and collect known-good
  samples from the same domain before inferring anything.

The consequence: **collect more known-good samples per domain in India than you
would elsewhere** — three or four rather than two — because a single sample
from a Western-named colleague will confidently produce the wrong pattern for
an Indian-named target.

## Calling an Indian company

The phone still works in Indian B2B, considerably better than in most Western
markets, and a switchboard call is often faster than a week of email.

Two constraints:

- **TRAI DND / DNC registry.** Applies to promotional calls and SMS to Indian
  numbers. A one-to-one business call to a company landline about a specific
  proposal is a different thing from a promotional campaign, but any campaign
  activity needs the registry checked and the sender registered under TRAI's
  commercial-communication framework. `references/compliance.md` has the
  detail.
- **Timing.** Business hours are genuinely observed; calls before 10:00 or
  during the 13:00–14:30 window land badly, and the regional festival calendar
  materially affects who is at their desk. Diwali week is not the week.
