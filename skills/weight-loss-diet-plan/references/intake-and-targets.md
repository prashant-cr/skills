## The full question set

Group them like this and send as one message. The grouping matters — it reads as a form to fill
in rather than an interview, and people complete forms.

**The body**

1. Age, sex, height, current weight.
2. Body fat percentage, if they happen to know it from a scale or a scan. Optional.
3. Goal weight, or goal in general terms. Any date attached to it.
4. Weight history — highest, lowest, and how the weight arrived. Someone who gained 15 kg in two
   years since a job change has a different problem from someone who has carried it since
   childhood.

**Movement**

5. Job — seated, standing, physical? Roughly how many steps on a normal day, if their phone tells
   them.
6. Deliberate exercise — what, how many days a week, how hard, and how long has it been going on?
7. Resistance training specifically, yes or no. This changes the protein conversation and how
   much muscle they'll keep.

**Food as it is now**

8. Walk me through what you ate yesterday, or a normal weekday — everything, including drinks and
   what went in the coffee.
9. And a normal weekend day, because these usually differ enough to matter.
10. Meal timing — when is the first meal, when is the last, is there a long gap or constant
    grazing?
11. What are you drinking? Water, tea, coffee with what in it, soft drinks, alcohol and how often.

**Preferences and constraints**

12. Diet pattern — vegetarian, vegan, eggetarian, non-vegetarian, pescatarian, halal, kosher,
    jain, or something else. Ask outright.
13. Allergies and intolerances.
14. Foods you actively dislike or won't eat.
15. Cuisines you cook and eat most.

**Logistics**

16. Who cooks, and how much time is available on a weekday evening?
17. Do you carry lunch, or eat out or order in? How often?
18. Any budget ceiling.
19. Kitchen — fridge and freezer space, microwave, oven, air fryer, blender?
20. Who else are you cooking for, and do they eat the same thing?

**Health**

21. Any diagnosed conditions — diabetes, thyroid, PCOS, blood pressure, cholesterol, gut
    conditions, anything else.
22. Any medications or supplements.
23. Anything relevant about your history with dieting, including anything that went badly.

**Life**

24. Sleep — hours, and quality.
25. Stress and work pattern — shifts, travel, unpredictable hours.

Questions 1, 5, 6, 8, 12, 13 and 21 are the ones you genuinely cannot plan without. Everything
else improves the plan; none of it should block producing one.

## Asking well

**Send it once.** Twenty-five questions one at a time is an interrogation and people leave. In one
block with light headings, most people answer most of it.

**Say why briefly.** "So the plan is built around your actual week rather than a generic one" is
enough. It measurably improves the quality of the answers, particularly on the food-recall
questions.

**Don't re-ask what they've told you.** If the conversation already contains their weight or that
they're vegetarian, reflect it back for confirmation and ask only for the gaps.

**Let them skip.** Say the optional ones are optional. A partial intake plus stated assumptions
beats a second round of questions, and beats no plan at all.

**Notice what's underneath.** Someone giving a deadline and a large target ("12 kg before the
wedding in eight weeks") is telling you the plan will be judged against an impossible standard.
Address the arithmetic early and kindly rather than producing a plan they'll consider a failure
by week three.

## Calibrating activity level

Self-reported activity is the single largest source of error in the whole calculation, and it
skews high almost universally — people count intentions, count their most active week, and count
exercise sessions as bigger than they are.

Map what they say onto the categories rather than asking them to self-classify:

| What they describe | Category |
| --- | --- |
| Desk job, no regular exercise, drives everywhere, under ~5,000 steps | `sedentary` |
| Desk job plus 2-3 gym sessions, or lots of walking, ~7,500 steps | `lightly_active` |
| Training 3-5 days consistently, or a job on their feet, ~10,000 steps | `moderately_active` |
| Training 6-7 days, or manual work, or both | `very_active` |
| Manual job plus daily training, or an athlete in season | `extra_active` |

Two rules that save trouble. **When the description sits between two, take the lower one** — a
target that turns out slightly conservative shows up as faster-than-expected loss, which is a
pleasant problem to fix. And **don't count exercise twice**: the multiplier already includes
training, so adding "eat back" calories on gym days on top of a `moderately_active` multiplier
double-counts and stalls people.

The multiplier is an estimate with real error bars either way. Say so, and frame the first two
weeks as the calibration: the scale trend is the measurement, the formula was only the starting
guess.

## When they won't or can't give numbers

Some people don't own scales, don't know their height in the units you're using, or don't want to
share a weight. You can still be useful.

- **No weight** — you cannot compute a deficit. But you can build a plan structured around protein
  per meal, volume and fibre, and describe portions by hand size (palm of protein, fist of
  vegetables, cupped hand of carbohydrate, thumb of fat). This works genuinely well and needs no
  numbers at all.
- **No weighing scale for food** — use household measures and hand sizes throughout, and don't
  pretend to a precision that isn't there.
- **Reluctant to discuss weight** — take the hint. Build the plan around food quality, protein and
  meal structure, and drop the calorie framing entirely. Pushing for a number here is both rude
  and, for anyone with a difficult history around food, harmful.

## Reading the current diet

The food-recall answer is the highest-value thing in the intake and it repays careful reading.
Look for:

- **Where the calories actually are.** Usually a small number of items: the cooking oil, the
  sweetened drinks, the evening snacking, the portion size of rice or bread. Three targeted
  changes often cover the whole deficit without restructuring anything.
- **Protein at breakfast.** Overwhelmingly the weakest meal, and the easiest large win.
- **The gap before dinner.** A long unfed stretch ending in the biggest meal of the day predicts
  evening overeating better than almost anything else.
- **Weekend drift.** Two days of significant overshoot erases five days of a moderate deficit. If
  the weekday and weekend answers diverge sharply, that gap *is* the problem — say so directly.
- **What they clearly enjoy.** Keep it. A plan that removes everything a person likes has a short
  half-life, and there is almost always a way to fit the thing they enjoy into the budget.

## Special populations

**PCOS** — no special calorie maths needed, but protein and fibre help with the insulin
resistance that often accompanies it, and weight loss is genuinely harder. Set expectations
accordingly and don't let them conclude they're failing.

**Hypothyroid, treated and stable** — normal calculation. Untreated or unstable, the maintenance
figure is unreliable; suggest they get levels checked first. Note that levothyroxine wants an
empty stomach and separation from calcium and iron by about four hours.

**Type 2 diabetes** — a deficit and better food quality both help substantially. If on insulin or
a sulfonylurea, doses may need review before carbohydrate drops. Metformin alone carries no
hypoglycaemia risk.

**Over 65** — protein needs go **up**, not down (sarcopenia risk rises in a deficit), and the rate
of loss should be gentler. Resistance training matters more here than at any other age.

**Shift workers** — meal timing advice built around a standard day is useless. Anchor meals to
their waking hours rather than the clock, and put the protein and volume in the part of the shift
where hunger actually hits.

**Ramadan or other fasting periods** — the eating window is fixed, so plan two or three substantial
protein-anchored meals inside it, prioritise hydration, and drop the deficit to a smaller one or to
maintenance for the duration.

## What the targets mean

When you present the numbers, explain them once so they aren't treated as arbitrary:

- **Maintenance** is an estimate, not a measurement. The first fortnight of scale data is what
  turns it into a real number.
- **Target calories** is the day's budget. Slightly over is not a failure; the weekly average is
  what moves weight.
- **Protein** is a floor to reach, not a cap to stay under. This is the number to protect if the
  day goes sideways.
- **Fat** is a minimum for hormonal health, not a target.
- **Carbohydrate** is the flexible remainder. It's where you spend what's left, and it's the
  right lever for training days.
- **Fibre** is the satiety budget, and the reason the plan is survivable.
