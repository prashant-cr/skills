## Measure before you adjust

Almost every bad adjustment comes from reacting to noise.

Scale weight moves 1-3 kg within a day on water, sodium, carbohydrate intake, bowel contents and
where a woman is in her cycle. A single weigh-in carries almost no information about fat mass. So
the protocol is:

- **Weigh daily**, same conditions — morning, after the toilet, before eating.
- **Compare seven-day averages, week to week.** Never compare two individual days.
- **Expect flat or upward weeks inside a genuine downward trend.** They are normal, not evidence.
- **Track something besides the scale** — waist measurement, how clothes fit, gym performance,
  monthly photos. During the first fortnight, and for anyone new to resistance training, the scale
  is the least informative of these.

If someone weighs weekly rather than daily, treat their data as much noisier and be correspondingly
slower to change anything.

**For women**, the cycle can add or remove 1-2 kg of water, most often in the luteal phase. The
cleanest read is to compare the same phase across months. Someone panicking about a 1.5 kg gain in
premenstrual week needs this explained, not a smaller calorie target.

## The weekly decision rules

Comparing this week's seven-day average against last week's, as a percentage of bodyweight:

| Weekly change | Read | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Losing more than 1% | Too fast — muscle loss and adherence risk | Add 100-150 kcal, mostly carbohydrate |
| Losing 0.4-1% | Working as designed | Change nothing |
| Losing 0.1-0.4% | Slow but real | Hold two more weeks before touching anything |
| Flat for 2 weeks | Investigate before cutting | See diagnosing a stall, below |
| Flat for 3+ weeks with confirmed adherence | Genuine plateau | Cut 100-150 kcal, or add 1,500-2,000 daily steps |
| Gaining, with confirmed adherence | Intake estimate was low | Recheck the food log honestly before anything else |

Three principles hold this together. **Wait two weeks before acting** — one week is noise.
**Change one thing at a time**, or you learn nothing about what worked. And **prefer adding
activity to subtracting food**, because there is a floor under how little someone can eat and no
equivalent ceiling on how much they can walk.

## Diagnosing a stall

Work through this in order. The order matters, because cutting calories on a plan that is already
being under-eaten makes hunger, adherence and muscle loss all worse simultaneously.

**1. Is it water?** Recent hard training, a high-sodium meal, a big carbohydrate refeed, new
creatine, poor sleep or menstrual timing can each mask two weeks of fat loss. If waist measurement
or clothes are still moving while the scale isn't, this is what's happening. Wait.

**2. Is it adherence drift?** This is the answer most of the time, and it is rarely deliberate.
Portions creep upward, oil stops being measured, the handful of nuts and the tastes while cooking
go uncounted, the weekend is quietly 800 kcal over. Ask them to log four days including a weekend,
weighing what they eat — not as a punishment, as measurement. Most stalls resolve here.

**3. Has activity dropped?** Non-exercise movement falls in a deficit — it is one of the body's
genuine adaptations, and it is largely unconscious. Someone who walked 9,000 steps in week one may
be at 6,000 by week six without noticing. Step data usually shows it plainly.

**4. Has maintenance fallen?** It has, somewhat — a smaller body costs less to run, and there is a
modest adaptive component on top. This is real but it is small, and it is the *last* explanation to
reach for, not the first. Recompute maintenance at the new bodyweight before assuming anything
exotic. "Damaged metabolism" and "starvation mode" as commonly described are not real; the honest
version is that maintenance is now lower and the deficit needs to be re-established.

## Losing too fast

Faster is not better, and someone dropping 1.5% of bodyweight a week is not ahead of schedule.

At that rate a rising share of the loss is lean tissue, hunger becomes the day's main event,
training performance falls, and the odds of abandoning the whole thing rise steeply. Add calories
back. Say why — people are usually reluctant, because fast loss feels like success.

The exception worth naming: **the first week is often dramatic**, 2-3 kg, and most of it is
glycogen and its associated water rather than fat. Say this in advance, because the following
week's much smaller number otherwise reads as failure.

## Diet breaks

Any deficit run for months has a psychological cost that accumulates quietly, and the failure mode
is not a bad day — it is abandonment.

A structured break addresses that: **one to two weeks at maintenance calories, every 8-12 weeks of
dieting.** Keep protein up, keep training, raise carbohydrate to meet maintenance. Expect the
scale to rise 1-2 kg from water and food volume; say so in advance or the break will read as
backsliding.

This is not a cheat week. It is planned maintenance, tracked, and it is one of the better
predictors of whether someone is still going at month six. For anyone dieting more than about
twelve weeks, put it in the plan from the start rather than offering it as a rescue later.

## Repairing adherence

When someone says they can't stick to the plan, the plan is the thing to change. This is the most
important instinct in the whole skill, and it cuts directly against how most diet advice responds.

Diagnose which failure it actually is:

| What they report | What it usually means | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hungry all the time | Deficit too aggressive, or protein and fibre too low | Raise protein and volume first; ease the deficit if that doesn't do it |
| Fine all day, then binge at night | Under-eating earlier, or an unfed gap before dinner | Redistribute calories later; add an afternoon protein snack |
| Weekdays fine, weekends undone | The plan has no weekend | Plan the weekend explicitly, including the meal out |
| No time to cook | Plan assumed a kitchen life they don't have | More batch cooking, fewer distinct dishes, more assembly-only meals |
| Bored of the food | Rotation too narrow, or too bland | Same components, different seasoning; add two dinners |
| Keep forgetting / drifting | No structure | Fixed meal times, a fixed shopping day, a named fallback |
| Fine but the scale isn't moving | Measurement problem, not a will problem | Go back to diagnosing a stall |

Two things to hold onto. **Do not respond to a struggling person by shrinking the plan** — that is
the reflex, and it makes every one of the rows above worse. And **treat a broken week as data**:
what specifically happened, on which day, and what would have made it survivable. That question
produces a better plan. "Try harder" does not.

## When to stop dieting

Worth raising proactively, because most people don't have an endpoint in mind and the endpoint is
where the weight comes back.

Reasons to end a deficit and move to maintenance:

- The goal has been reached, or a sensible one has.
- Twelve to sixteen weeks of continuous dieting have passed — take a break, then decide.
- Sleep, mood, libido, training performance or menstrual regularity have deteriorated. These are
  the body's actual signals and they are worth more than the target.
- Food is becoming an anxiety rather than a task. Stop, and if it persists, suggest they talk to
  a professional.
- Life has become genuinely unsuitable — a bereavement, a new baby, a crisis at work. Maintaining
  through a hard period is a real success, and framing it that way prevents the all-or-nothing
  collapse.

## Finishing

The transition out matters as much as the diet, and it is where almost all regain happens.

Move to maintenance **deliberately**: add roughly 100-150 kcal a week until the weight holds
steady, mostly as carbohydrate. This takes several weeks and it is a plan of its own, not an
absence of one.

Then keep the parts that were structural rather than restrictive — the protein target, the
resistance training, the vegetable volume, the batch cooking, the weekly weigh-in average. Those
transfer to maintenance unchanged and cost nothing.

Set a **re-entry trigger** before it's needed: a weight at which they'll act, say 3 kg above the
new one. Catching 3 kg is a fortnight's work. Catching 12 kg is starting again, and starting again
is the pattern this is trying to break.
