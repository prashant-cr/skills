## The one number that sorts every food

Not grams of protein per 100 g — **grams of protein per 100 kcal**. Because in a deficit the
calorie budget is the scarce resource, and that ratio tells you how much protein a food buys you
per unit of the thing you're short of.

It reorganises the usual intuitions immediately. Peanuts and almonds are widely thought of as
protein foods; they are fat foods that contain some protein, and 100 g of almonds costs a third of
a day's calories to deliver 21 g. Full-fat paneer is a protein food in reputation and a fat food
in practice. Egg whites, which sound like a diet fad, are one of the most protein-dense foods
available anywhere.

A rough reading of the scale:

| Protein per 100 kcal | What it is | Role in the plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 g and above | A protein food | Anchor meals on these |
| 8-15 g | A genuine contributor | Fills the gaps, adds variety |
| 4-8 g | A carb or fat food containing protein | Count it, don't rely on it |
| Below 4 g | Not a protein source | Include for taste, fibre, enjoyment |

Someone eating 1,500 kcal and needing 130 g of protein needs an average of 8.7 g per 100 kcal
**across the whole day**. That is a demanding average — it means most of the day has to come from
the top two rows, with the bottom rows as the accompaniment rather than the base. Showing someone
this number explains, better than any lecture, why their current diet was never going to work.

## What this means per diet pattern

**Non-vegetarian** — straightforward. Two palm-sized portions of lean meat or fish plus dairy or
eggs will carry most targets. The failure mode is not protein, it's the cooking fat and the
portion size of the rice or bread alongside.

**Pescatarian** — same, with white fish and prawns being the most calorie-efficient and oily fish
worth keeping in twice a week for omega-3.

**Eggetarian** — eggs plus dairy makes this comfortable. Whole eggs cost 72 kcal for 6 g, so
mixing whole eggs with extra whites (a 2-whole-plus-4-whites omelette gives 27 g for 215 kcal) is
the standard move for getting the protein without the calories.

**Vegetarian with dairy** — dairy does the heavy lifting. Greek yogurt, quark, skyr, cottage
cheese and low-fat paneer are the workhorses; full-fat paneer is fine but expensive in calories,
so use it for flavour rather than as the protein base. Legumes fill in, soy products cover
dinners.

**Vegan** — the hard case, covered below.

**Jain** — no root vegetables, often no onion or garlic. Protein comes from dairy if accepted,
plus legumes, tofu (check the source is acceptable), paneer, curd, and nuts and seeds in
moderation. Flavour is built on asafoetida, ginger, tomatoes and spices rather than the onion-
garlic base, so recipe suggestions have to be genuinely jain, not standard recipes with items
crossed out.

**Halal or kosher** — the protein sources are unchanged; sourcing is the constraint. Ask whether
they have reliable access to certified meat, and if not, weight the plan toward fish, eggs, dairy
and plant sources rather than writing a plan around meat they can't easily buy.

## The vegan arithmetic problem

This deserves stating plainly, because it is where most plant-based fat-loss plans quietly fail.

Take someone targeting 1,600 kcal and 120 g of protein. If they build from lentils at 8 g per 100
kcal, hitting 120 g of protein costs 1,500 kcal — essentially the whole day, with nothing left
for vegetables, fat or any carbohydrate that isn't a lentil. The plan is arithmetically impossible
and it is not obvious until you total it, which is why `scripts/plan_check.py` exists.

The way out is to lean on the concentrated end rather than eating more of the same:

- **Soy in its dense forms.** Rehydrated soy chunks and seitan sit at 17-18 g per 100 kcal, right
  alongside chicken breast. These are what make a vegan deficit workable.
- **Tofu and tempeh** at 10 as the everyday base for dinners.
- **Legumes as the middle layer**, not the foundation — they bring fibre, cost and texture, and
  contribute real protein, but they cannot carry the target alone.
- **A protein powder** for the last 20-30 g. There is no nutritional prize for refusing it, and
  the plans that hit their targets usually include one.

One secondary point on amino acids: plant proteins are individually lower in specific essential
amino acids, but this resolves itself across a varied day — the old "combine at every meal" rule is
not supported and is not worth the complexity.

### The micronutrients that actually need attention

A vegan diet and a calorie deficit are each survivable; together they squeeze the same few
nutrients. Generic "watch your iron" is useless, so give the actionable version:

- **B12** — supplementation is necessary, not optional. Not a preference, not covered by a varied
  diet. Say it plainly.
- **Iron** — the non-haem form absorbs poorly. Pair iron-containing meals with vitamin C (lemon,
  tomato, capsicum, citrus) and keep tea and coffee away from those meals by an hour or so, since
  the tannins meaningfully inhibit absorption. This timing change is free and does more than
  switching foods.
- **Calcium** — buy **fortified** plant milk and yogurt and check the label, because unfortified
  versions have almost none. This is the easiest gap to open by accident, since the two products
  look identical on the shelf.
- **Iodine** — plant diets are commonly low. Iodised salt or a supplement; seaweed works but the
  dose is wildly variable, so it's a poor primary source.
- **Omega-3** — flax, chia and walnuts give ALA, which converts to EPA and DHA inefficiently. An
  algal oil supplement is the direct route if they want one.
- **Zinc** — legumes and seeds cover it, but phytates reduce absorption; soaking, sprouting and
  fermenting help.

### Hidden animal ingredients

Worth a short list, because a plan can be scrupulously vegan on its own terms and still get
undone by a restaurant dish or a packet. The ones that catch people:

- **Miso soup and most Japanese broths** — normally made with bonito (fish) dashi, including
  instant sachets. Ask for or buy a kombu-only version.
- **Dal and Indian gravies** — frequently finished with ghee, cream or a cashew-cream paste; naan
  usually contains milk or yogurt. A dal that reads vegan on the menu often isn't.
- **Thai and South-East Asian dishes** — fish sauce and shrimp paste are in a great many curry
  pastes and dressings.
- **Bread and baked goods** — whey, milk powder and eggwash are common.
- **Anything gelled** — gelatin in sweets, marshmallows, some yogurts and capsules.
- **Parmesan and similar hard cheeses** — animal rennet, so not vegetarian either, which surprises
  long-time vegetarians.
- **Worcestershire sauce, Caesar dressing, some kimchi** — anchovy.
- **Wine, beer and cider** — occasionally fined with isinglass or gelatin.

For a vegetarian rather than vegan plan, the rennet and gelatin items are the ones that matter;
the dairy and egg entries obviously don't.

## Swapping without recalculating

Give the person a swap list, not just a plan, because the plan they follow will diverge from the
plan you wrote and you want the divergence to land somewhere sensible.

The trick is to express swaps in **portions that match on protein**, so they can trade freely
without doing arithmetic:

```
Any of these is "one protein block" — roughly 25 g protein, 130-180 kcal
  Chicken breast          85 g
  Prawns or white fish    110 g
  Tuna, canned            100 g
  Egg whites              225 g (about 7)
  Greek yogurt 0%         250 g
  Low-fat paneer          140 g
  Seitan                  100 g
  Soy chunks, rehydrated  140 g
  Whey or pea protein     1 scoop
```

Then the plan reads "three protein blocks a day plus your meals", and a missed ingredient stops
being a reason to abandon the day.

## Satiety and fibre

Fullness per calorie is what makes a deficit survivable, and it tracks three things: protein,
fibre, and water content. That ranks foods roughly like this — potatoes, oats, legumes, fruit,
lean meat, fish, eggs, yogurt and vegetables at the top; refined bread, pastries, chocolate, oils
and nuts at the bottom.

The practical levers:

- **Volume first.** A large salad or a bowl of broth-based soup before the main meal reliably
  reduces total intake, and costs almost nothing from the budget.
- **Fibre to target.** 14 g per 1,000 kcal. Vegetables, whole fruit, legumes, whole grains, chia
  and flax. If you raise fibre sharply, say so — going from 12 g to 30 g overnight is
  uncomfortable and people blame the diet.
- **Whole fruit over juice**, always. Same sugar, entirely different satiety.
- **Watch the liquid calories.** Sweetened coffee, juice, smoothies and alcohol are the quiet
  third of the budget in a lot of people's current intake, and cutting them is usually the least
  painful large saving available.

## Supplements

Almost none are needed. Worth mentioning only when they earn it:

- **Protein powder** — not a supplement so much as a convenient food. Genuinely useful when the
  target is hard to hit, which is most vegan plans and most people over about 140 g.
- **B12** — necessary for vegans, and worth checking for long-term vegetarians.
- **Vitamin D** — reasonable in low-sun latitudes and winter, for anyone.
- **Creatine** — well evidenced for anyone doing resistance training, and helps retain strength
  in a deficit. Mention the initial water-weight gain of 1-2 kg or they will think the diet has
  failed in week one.
- **Fat burners, detox teas, apple cider vinegar, "metabolism boosters"** — say clearly that these
  do not work. People ask, and a straight answer saves them money.
